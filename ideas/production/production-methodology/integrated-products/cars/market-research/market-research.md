## Part 1

From conversation with Gemini on [how many people in U.S. own a car](https://share.google/aimode/J8WtSA2zIUcs7UjdV)

> how many people in U.S. own a car?

While the U.S. government tracks vehicle ownership by household rather than individual people, **roughly 92% of U.S. households own at least one vehicle**, and there are **approximately 298 million registered motor vehicles** nationwide.

Because households often share cars and some individuals own multiple vehicles, ownership is best understood through a few key metrics:

- **Household Ownership**. About 91.5% to 92% of American households have access to at least one personal vehicle, leaving only around 8% to 8.5% of households car-free.
- **Vehicles Per Household**. The average U.S. household owns about 1.83 vehicles. Nearly 38% of households own two vehicles, and over 20% own three or more.
- **Total Vehicles vs. Drivers**. According to the [Federal Highway Administration](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/policyinformation/pubs/our_nations_highways_2026/vehicles.cfm), there are 298 million registered motor vehicles in the U.S. (which includes cars, SUVs, pickup trucks, vans, and motorcycles) compared to about 240 million licensed drivers.
- **Vehicles Per Capita**. This equals roughly 88 vehicles for every 100 U.S. residents, giving the United States one of the highest rates of vehicle ownership in the world.


### Regional Differences

Ownership varies sharply depending on location and access to public transit, as detailed in [The Motley Fool's car ownership research](https://www.fool.com/money/research/car-ownership-statistics/):
- **Highest Ownership**. Rural states like Idaho and Wyoming have the highest household ownership rates at roughly 96%.
- **Lowest Ownership**. Dense urban areas with extensive public transit have the lowest rates, such as Washington, D.C. (around 64%) and New York City (around 45.6%).

## Part 2

From conversation with Gemini on [who are the 8% that don't own cars?](https://share.google/aimode/ek3m6gkY4WSBIeIBT)

> who are the 8% that don't own cars?

The roughly 8% to 8.8% of U.S. households without a car (about 11 to 12 million households) fall into two very different groups: **those who cannot afford or physically operate a vehicle, and those who live in dense cities where a car is unnecessary**.

According to data from the  and [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/11/14/1-in-10-americans-rarely-or-never-drive-a-car/), car-free households are heavily concentrated among a few overlapping demographics:


### 1. Low-Income Households ("Carless by Necessity")

Income is the single strongest predictor of whether a household owns a car. Because buying, insuring, fueling, and maintaining a vehicle is expensive, millions of low-income families are priced out of ownership:

- **Under $15,000/year**. Over 41% of households in this bracket do not own a vehicle.
- **Middle vs. Low Income**. By comparison, only about 2.4% of households earning between $75,000 and $150,000 go without a car.
- **Racial Disparities**. Because of overlapping income gaps and higher concentrations in urban centers, Black households (roughly 21% to 23% car-free) and Hispanic households (12% to 14%) are much more likely to be without a vehicle than White households (around 7%).

### 2. Dense Urban Residents ("Car-Free by Choice")

In walkable cities with extensive subways, buses, and commuter rail, owning a car is often more of a financial burden and parking headache than a convenience:

- **Transit Hubs**. New York City alone accounts for a massive portion of the nation's car-free population—over half of NYC households (around 52.5%) do not own a car. Other cities with high car-free rates include Washington, D.C. (38%), Philadelphia (22.5%), Chicago (20.8%), Boston (17.2%), and San Francisco (15%).
- **High-Income Urbanites**. While carlessness drops sharply as income rises, it actually ticks back up slightly among households earning $150,000 or more (to around 3%), reflecting affluent city dwellers who rely on transit, walking, bikes, and rideshares.


### 3. Older Adults and People with Disabilities

- **Seniors**. Older adults—particularly single seniors living alone—make up a significant share of zero-vehicle households, often giving up driving due to vision, mobility, or cognitive changes.
- **Disabilities**. Millions of working-age and older Americans have medical conditions or disabilities that prevent them from driving safely or legally.

### 4. Young Adults and College Students

- **Under 30**. Roughly 15% of adults under age 30 rarely or never drive. Many live in college towns or urban neighborhoods, delay getting driver's licenses compared to previous generations, or rely on campus transit and micro-mobility (e-bikes and scooters).

From conversation with Gemini on [how many people would likely own a car](https://share.google/aimode/8m5OuT0JPhY61quma)

> how many people would likely own a car, would there be good enough conditions for it, e.g.:
> - low enough price so they can afford (assume super cheap car, what the price should be?)

If cars became ultra-cheap, U.S. household ownership would likely climb from ~91.2% to a **realistic ceiling of 95% to 96%**, adding roughly **5 to 6.5 million households** (out of the ~11.8 million currently car-free).

Even if a car were nearly free, universal 100% ownership wouldn't happen because non-financial barriers—like age, disability, and dense urban infrastructure—keep millions of Americans off the road.

### 1. What Price Would Make a Car 'Super Cheap' Enough?

To bring in the lowest-income households (those earning under $15,000–$25,000/year), a new car would need to cost **$5,000 to $8,000** — roughly the price of a high-end e-bike, used motorcycle, or ultra-compact neighborhood electric vehicle (like a Citroën Ami or Wuling Hongguang Mini EV).

- **The Sticker Price Trap**. A standard budget car today costs ~$20,000–$25,000. At $6,000 with a small down payment, a 60-month loan would drop to roughly **$100–$115/month** (compared to the average U.S. car payment of over $600/month).
- **The Hidden Hurdle (Operating Costs)**. Even if the sticker price dropped to $5,000, AAA's Your Driving Costs study shows that fixed and operating expenses still add up fast. Insurance (~$2,100/year), fuel, maintenance, and registration easily cost **$3,500 to $4,500 a year** ($300–$375/month).
- **The True Affordability Threshold**. Transportation economists consider a car "affordable" when total costs stay under **10% to 15% of gross household income**. For a household making $20,000/year, their *entire* car budget (loan + gas + insurance) is **$165 to $250/month**. Unless the car were a tiny, low-speed electric microcar with minimal insurance and near-zero fuel costs, even a $5,000 gas car would still stretch a poverty-level budget.

### 2. How Many of the 11.8 Million Car-Free Households Would Buy One?

According to , there are about **11.8 million zero-vehicle households** in the U.S. (8.8% of all 134 million households). Here is how an ultra-cheap car would affect them:

- **Who Would Buy (~5 to 6.5 million households)**. Roughly half of car-free households are "carless by necessity". This includes low-income workers in suburbs, small towns, and rural areas (over 1 million rural households lack a car), as well as lower-income city residents working late shifts or jobs unreachable by bus. A $5,000–$7,000 reliable vehicle would immediately unlock car ownership for most of this group.
- **Who Still Wouldn't Buy (~5.5 to 6.8 million households)**.
	- **Dense Transit Cities (~3 to 3.5 million households)**. In places like New York City (where 2.4 million households lack a car), San Francisco, or D.C., the barrier isn't the car's price—it's **$300–$600/month parking**, heavy traffic, and fast subways.
	- **Seniors & People with Disabilities (~2 to 2.5 million households)**. Millions of elderly Americans living alone and people with visual, cognitive, or physical disabilities cannot legally or safely drive at any price.
	- **Students & Minimalists (~0.5 to 1 million households)**. College students in walkable campus towns or young urbanites who prefer e-bikes, transit, and rideshares.


### 3. The Biggest Secondary Effect: Multi-Car Households

While an ultra-cheap car would only push total household ownership up by **4 to 5 percentage points** (to ~96%, matching rural states like Wyoming and Idaho), it would cause a massive spike in **two- and three-car households**. Millions of single-car families who currently share a vehicle due to cost would likely buy a second "commuter" microcar if it cost $5,000.

## Part 4

From conversation with Gemini on [computer ownership in U.S.](https://share.google/aimode/ia6WMG4Fk6hhmiStv)

About **80.5% of U.S. households own a traditional desktop or laptop computer** (roughly **106.9 million households**), while **96.6% own at least one type of computing device** when smartphones and tablets are included.

Because the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://data.census.gov/table?tid=ACSDT1Y2024.B28010) classifies smartphones and tablets as "computers," the numbers look very different depending on how you define the term:

- **Desktop or Laptop Owners (~80.5%)**. Roughly 106.9 million out of 132.7 million U.S. households have a traditional PC or laptop at home.
- **Any Computing Device (~96.6%)**. About 128.2 million households own at least one device—a desktop, laptop, smartphone, or tablet.
- **Smartphone-Only Households (~10% to 16%)**. Roughly 13.2 million households rely *exclusively* on a smartphone to go online and do not own a laptop, desktop, or tablet.
- **No Computing Device at All (~3.4%)**. Only about 4.5 million U.S. households have no computer, tablet, or smartphone whatsoever.


### Who Doesn't Own a Traditional Computer (and Why)?

Similar to car ownership, the roughly **26 million households without a desktop or laptop** are split between economic barriers and lifestyle substitutes—though with computers, **smartphones act as the "public transit" substitute**:

- **Low-Income & "Smartphone-Dependent" Households**. Just like a cheap car still requires gas and insurance, a laptop requires **home broadband internet** (~$60–$85/month). According to [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/08/internet-use-smartphone-ownership-digital-divides-in-u-s/), 34% of adults earning under $30,000 rely solely on their smartphone's cellular plan instead of paying for both a PC and home Wi-Fi.
- **Older Adults (65+)**. Seniors living alone make up the largest share of the 3.4% with zero devices, often due to unfamiliarity with technology, vision issues, or feeling they have no daily need for one.
- **Mobile-First Young Adults**. Many Gen Z and younger adults do almost everything—banking, shopping, streaming, and social media—on a smartphone or tablet, skipping a traditional PC unless required for school or remote work.


### How Cheap Would a Computer Have to Be for Everyone to Own One?

Computers have already gone through the exact "ultra-cheap" price drop we discussed for cars—entry-level Chromebooks and refurbished laptops routinely sell for **$100 to $200**.

That real-world experiment proves the same rule applies to both cars and computers: **dropping the hardware price near zero pushes ownership to roughly 96%–97%, but never to 100%**. Even when schools and nonprofits give away free laptops, the final few percent still don't use them due to **ongoing subscription costs (home internet), lack of need (smartphones cover daily tasks)**, or **age and accessibility barriers**.

From conversation with Gemini on [potential for commercial rocket market](https://share.google/aimode/ifGlBMS0CqCOK65Kz)

> what about rockets market? is there a potential for rockets would their price be cheap?

**Yes—the commercial rocket market is growing rapidly, and launch prices are on track to drop by another 10x or more**, shifting rockets from rare, billion-dollar government projects into routine cargo transport.

However, unlike cars or computers, rockets will almost certainly remain a **B2B and government service market** (buying cargo space or tickets) rather than something individual consumers buy and park in a driveway.

### 1. How Cheap Are Rockets Getting?

The cost of sending 1 kilogram of cargo to Low Earth Orbit (LEO) has already collapsed by over 90% thanks to reusable boosters, and the next generation of fully reusable rockets aims to push it down another order of magnitude, according to [Orbital Radar's launch cost tracker](https://orbitalradar.com/space-economy/launch-cost-trends) and [Orbital.Intel's historical analysis](https://orbital-intel.com/launch-cost-history/):


| Era / Rocket | Cost per kg to LEO | Typical Launch Price | Key Breakthrough |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Space Shuttle Era** (1981–2011) | ~$54,500 / kg | ~$1.5 billion | Partial reuse, massive refurbishment cost |
| **SpaceX Falcon 9** (Current) | ~$1,500–$3,000 / kg | ~$70–$74 million | Reusable first-stage booster (20+ flights) |
| **Small Rideshare** (Transporter) | ~$6,000–$7,000 / kg | ~$350,000 (for 50 kg) | Shared "bus" rides for tiny satellites |
| **SpaceX Starship** (Target) | **$100–$200 / kg** | ~$10–$20 million (est.) | 100% rapid reusability + massive 150t+ payload |

- **Why prices are falling**: In the past, throwing away a rocket after one flight was like flying a Boeing 747 from New York to London and scrapping the plane on arrival. Once a rocket is 100% reusable like an airliner, the main recurring cost becomes **propellant (liquid methane and oxygen)**, which costs only a few hundred thousand dollars per flight.
- **The "Magic Number" ($200/kg)**: Industry analysts note that dropping launch costs below **$200/kg** unlocks entirely new businesses—like orbital data centers, space-based solar power, microgravity pharmaceutical manufacturing, and point-to-point Earth cargo.


### 2. How Big Is the Market Potential?

While the rocket launch industry itself is relatively small today, it acts as the "railroad" that enables the massive broader space economy:

- **Launch Services Market**: Valued around **$10 to $14 billion**, projected to roughly double to **$24–$28 billion by the early 2030s** as competitors like Rocket Lab (Neutron), Blue Origin (New Glenn), and Relativity Space scale up.
- **Total Space Economy**: Currently worth roughly **$626 to $690 billion** and projected to surpass **$1 trillion by the early-to-mid 2030s** (driven by satellite internet like Starlink, Earth observation, defense, and navigation).

### 3. Why Rockets Won't Become Like Cars or PCs

Even if launch prices hit $100/kg, rockets face hard physical and regulatory ceilings that keep them out of everyday consumer ownership:

- **Physics & Energy**: Escaping Earth's gravity requires accelerating to ~28,000 km/h (17,500 mph). That takes thousands of tons of explosive cryogenic fuel—something that cannot be miniaturized safely for a backyard.
- **Noise, Blast Radius & Airspace**: A heavy rocket launch produces shockwaves that can shatter windows miles away and requires clearing commercial airspace and shipping lanes.
- **The "Airline" Model**: Instead of everyone owning a rocket, the market is evolving toward **commercial airlines and shipping containers**. Consumers and startups won't own rockets—they will buy a **$300,000 rideshare slot** to launch a satellite or eventually buy a **seat ticket** for suborbital/orbital travel or hypersonic point-to-point flights (e.g., New York to Tokyo in under an hour).
