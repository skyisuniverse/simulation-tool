**Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM)** is a **large-scale metal 3D printing process that uses an electric arc to melt metal wire and deposit it layer by layer**. Often guided by a robotic arm or CNC gantry, it builds near-net-shape metal parts and repairs existing components. Under ISO/ASTM 52900, it is classified as **directed energy deposition with an arc (DED-Arc)**.

## How It Works

WAAM pairs proven arc-welding hardware—a power source, wire feeder, and torch—with automated motion control and shielding gas to protect the molten pool. Common arc methods include GMAW/MIG, GTAW/TIG, PAW, and Cold Metal Transfer (CMT). [Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/wire-arc-additive-manufacturing), [Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing: Key insights](https://meltio3d.com/wire-arc-additive-manufacturing-key-insights/)

- **Design & slicing**: Engineers prepare the CAD model, plan toolpaths, and add extra material ("machining allowance") on surfaces that need precision finishing.
- **Layer-by-layer deposition**: The torch melts wire onto a base plate or existing part, fusing each layer to the one below.
- **Monitoring & finishing**: Sensors and thermal cameras track heat and geometry during printing. Most parts then go through heat treatment, stress relief, and CNC machining or grinding.

## Common Materials & Uses

- **Metals**: Steels, stainless steels, titanium alloys (like Ti-6Al-4V), aluminum alloys, magnesium alloys, and specialty/functionally graded alloys.

- **Applications**: Large structural parts and repairs in aerospace, maritime, defense, energy, and infrastructure—such as spars, landing gear components, ship/marine parts, tooling, and custom structural steelwork. [WAAM - Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/3HgGHtZJ7zY), [WAAM explained - Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing - RAMLAB](https://www.ramlab.com/resources/waam-101/)

## Key Pros & Trade-Offs

- **Pros**: High deposition rates (often around 1–10 kg/h), lower equipment and wire costs than laser/powder systems, minimal waste compared to machining from a solid block, and the ability to make parts several meters across.

- **Trade-offs**: Rougher surface finish and lower fine-feature detail than powder-bed methods, requiring post-machining on critical faces. High heat input also requires careful control to manage residual stress, distortion, and porosity.
